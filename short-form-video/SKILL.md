# Short-Form Video

Write every script for the **cold viewer**: a stranger mid-scroll who has never heard of this pharmacy, decides in three seconds, and whose trust at that moment sets the largest ask the video is allowed to make. Every beat below either buys trust or spends it.

Two branches: draft a script from a topic or an offer, or critique a script or a filmed cut that already exists.

## Gather the brand facts

A script cannot be finished from a topic alone. When `.agents/product-marketing.md` or `.claude/product-marketing.md` exists, read it first and ask only for what it leaves open — the marketing skills on this machine share that file. Collect:

- **Presenter** — name, credential, and the one number that makes them worth listening to.
- **Offer** — what the video points at, its price, and the exact link destination.
- **Temperature** — cold (organic reach, strangers) or warm (followers, past customers, a list). Organic short-form is cold unless the user says otherwise.
- **Constraints** — retail or compounding, and what this pharmacy is licensed and willing to say on camera.

Invented credentials and guessed prices are the failure mode here: both survive into a published video, and both are the pharmacy's liability rather than the drafter's. Ask for whatever is missing. This step is complete when presenter, offer, price, link, and temperature are known values rather than placeholders.

## Draft the four beats

Target 40–60 seconds, roughly 100–150 spoken words. One vertical 9:16 cut serves TikTok, Reels, Shorts, and Facebook video, with the differences small enough to handle in the handoff rather than the script:

| Platform | Sweet spot | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| TikTok | 15–60s | Rewards the rawer cut — a video shot at the counter over a staged one |
| Reels | 15–30s | Tightest; the story is what gets trimmed |
| YouTube Shorts | 30–60s | Search-indexed, so the title carries the words a patient would type |

Shorts is the one worth a second pass. It is the only platform here where someone finds the video months later by searching a question, so its title is written as that question.

### Hook — the first three seconds

Write three hooks in different styles, then pick one:

- **Problem-first** — "Most people take their thyroid medication wrong."
- **Polarizing** — "If you're buying magnesium at the grocery store, stop."
- **Curiosity gap** — "One patient tried this for 30 days. Here's her lab work."

One sentence, five to eight words. The script opens at full energy, because on a scroll there is no earlier line to build from — the first word is already the middle of the video.

The hook runs on three channels at once, and all three land inside the first second: what the presenter **says**, what the viewer **sees** in the opening frame, and the **text overlay** burned across it. Write all three. The overlay is not the spoken line transcribed — it is the same promise cut down to a thumbnail's worth of words, because a sound-off viewer reads it before hearing anything.

### Who — five to ten seconds

Name, role, and relevance in a single line: "I'm Dr. Jane Smith, a pharmacist who's worked with 2,000 patients on metabolic health." Use the real credential and the real number from the brand facts.

### Story — problem, action, result

One story, told the way it would be told across the counter: a de-identified patient case, the presenter's own experience, or the scenario this pharmacy sees every week. The pull is in the specific detail — what the person tried first, what changed, how long it took. Trade the clinical register for the counter register: "her energy came back around week three" over "symptomatic improvement was observed at week three."

### Close — on the back foot

Guide rather than push, in this order:

1. Recap the solution in one plain sentence.
2. Hand over the buying criteria they can use anywhere — "third-party tested, dosed at the amount that was actually studied, no fillers."
3. Name the product as something that meets those criteria.
4. Give one next step, sized by the section below.

The criteria are what make the close work. A viewer who now knows how to judge the whole category trusts the person who taught them, whether or not they buy today.

## Size the ask to the temperature

The call to action matches the viewer's relationship with the pharmacy rather than the margin on the product. Colder viewer, lower price and lower risk.

| Offer | Cold ask | Warm ask | Where the link goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single product | $30–$65 | $65–$100 | Product page |
| Bundle or kit | $100–$200 | $200–$300 | Product page, or a teaching video first |
| Test kit or program | Qualify first | $300+ | Quiz, form, or consult booking |

Above $200, a cold video sends viewers to a qualifier instead of a checkout: "Take the quiz to find the right plan," "Apply to see whether the program fits." Above $65 on a single product, the script pays for the gap with proof — a testimonial, a before and after, a comparison against what they would otherwise buy.

The link follows the same line: a direct product link inside the impulse range, a free resource for anything that needs trust first, both when the video genuinely teaches and sells. Social proof, urgency, and a clear transformation each stretch the workable range; price on its own is the weakest lever a video has.

A high price into a cold audience with no proof is the hardest combination there is. When the brand facts describe one, say so and script the trust-building video instead.

## Keep the script defensible

The presenter's license is what is on camera, so a script clears these before it is handed over:

- **Patient stories are de-identified to a local viewer's eye.** The HIPAA identifier list is the floor; the real test is a small-town audience. "A 62-year-old man who comes in on Tuesdays" is a name to anyone who shops there. Blur the specifics, build a composite, or work from a written authorization.
- **Supplements support; they do not treat.** Structure-function language — "supports healthy glucose metabolism" — stays inside what a supplement may claim. Disease language — treats, cures, prevents, reverses, lowers A1c — makes it an unapproved drug claim.
- **Compounded preparations stay described, not ranked.** They are not FDA-approved products, so the script explains what a compound is and who it suits rather than framing it as safer or more effective than the approved drug.
- **Testimonials carry the ordinary result.** A standout outcome gets the typical one said aloud in the same breath, and a paid or employed endorser is disclosed on screen.

This step is complete when every claim in the script is one the presenter could defend to their state board, and no person in the story is resolvable by someone who shops at that pharmacy.

## Hand over something shootable

Deliver the spoken lines with the four beats labelled and a running time, plus the setup card the presenter checks before recording:

- **Vertical 9:16**, framed from the chest up, with the head near the top of the frame rather than the middle.
- **Camera at eye level** — tilted up or down reads as looming or deferential, and both cost authority.
- **Light on the face from the front** — a window or a ring light, with the brightest thing in the room in front of the presenter rather than behind them.
- **A lav or headset mic, in a soft room** — audio carries a video that video cannot carry back, and a hard-surfaced room echoes.
- **A background chosen on purpose** — the shelf behind the presenter is either deliberate and on-brand, or empty.
- **Burned-in captions** — most of this feed is watched with the sound off, and a pharmacy's audience stacks sound-off viewing with hard-of-hearing viewers. Two lines on screen at most, three to five words a line, high-contrast sans with an outline, timed to the actual speech.

Ship the post copy beside the script: a first line that finishes its thought before the platform truncates it — about 125 characters is the safe fold — the next step written out for whoever reads instead of watches, and three to five specific hashtags. Stuffing has stopped working on all four platforms, and every tag still spends the caption's character budget.

Complete when the presenter could pick up a phone and record straight from the page without asking a question.

## Critique an existing video

Work from whatever exists: a draft script, a transcript, or the video file when this harness can open it. Production quality is judgeable only from the video itself — say so rather than inferring it from a script.

Rule on all six in order — hook, authority, story, close, ask-to-temperature, compliance — and for each either name what makes it clear the bar or rewrite it in place. A beat marked "weak" without a replacement line leaves the presenter exactly where they started.

When the video has already shipped, its numbers name the failing beat more reliably than a reading of the script does:

| What the numbers show | Which beat is failing |
|---|---|
| Views arrive, watch time dies in the first seconds | The hook |
| Watched through, but no follows or clicks | The close, or an ask the temperature will not carry |
| Saved often, rarely shared | Useful without being social — the story is thin or absent |
| Comments arriving hostile | The authority beat, or a claim the compliance pass should have caught |

Without numbers to work from, the hook is the highest-leverage single fix, and an ask too large for a cold viewer is the most common miss.

Complete when all six have been ruled on and every failing beat has its rewritten line sitting beside it.
